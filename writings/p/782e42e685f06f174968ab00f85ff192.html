Thoughts on Experiential Learning

With inspiration from biological intelligence

Oct 14, 26 · 17 min

“The most wonderful thing about [children] is their infinite capacity for wonder”— Alison Gopnik

The Bitter Lesson

Last September on the Dwarkesh Patel podcast, Richard Sutton explained the Bitter Lesson, arguing that methods for intelligence leveraging human data are consistently overtaken by those built on a system’s own experience.

Even at scale, approaches that rely primarily on existing data face a fundamental ceiling. Human data is finite, and producing more of it is slow and expensive. More importantly, each additional data sample relies on human aggregation and filtering and is not generated to maximize model learning. In contrast, experiential learning methods generate their own data through exploration and search. As a result, their progress is bounded only by compute, which has historically proven to scale far better and thus to be a much better strategy for intelligence.

AlphaGo, trained on the games of grandmasters, was beaten by AlphaZero, an experience-based method which learned by playing, losing, and winning. In Atari, methods built upon the rules of the game were beaten by experience-based methods.

In natural language, LLMs initially improved with the scale of data and model parameters. But now that most internet data has been consumed, research has returned to reinforcement and test-time learning. And this pattern, where experience vastly outperforms finite human data, has repeated itself across image processing, translation, and scientific understanding.

Still, I don't think this is understood, especially in embodied intelligence. In academic labs and industry, much of the hype surrounds methods built on human-derived priors like language or collecting human data through teleoperation, videos, and egocentric capture.

But if the goal is general physical intelligence— not just competence on certain, limited tasks— then these approaches are the exact trap Sutton warns about.

Animals Play

Young animals offer a useful example of experiential learning. They learn from exploration: playing, fighting, moving, falling, and trying new things.

A natural counterpoint against this is that many animals, like horses or giraffes, can walk almost immediately after birth. Indeed, this suggests the opposite of self-exploration: that genes encode a kind of “pre-training.” However, these built-in behaviors are more typical of animals with a lower encephalization quotient (EQ), or brain to body size. Low EQ animals are ready to act early, but their behavior is less flexible. On the other hand, animals1 with higher EQ like chimpanzees and dogs are the opposite. They are born far more helpless and dependent upon their guardians for longer, but gain the ability to adapt, generalize, and learn from experience.

Though this is correlation and not causal, it points to an interesting trend in nature: greater general intelligence seems to come with greater dependence on learning from experience. And importantly, this tradeoff isn’t neutral. Humans, at the extreme end of high EQ, dominate in general intelligence.

tl;dr: nature also supports the bitter lesson.

Babies Self-Collect to Learn

The most general learners among young animals are human infants. They are born with little2 hardcoded behavior and are vulnerable for years, yet they learn to build motor skills, recognize patterns, and predict outcomes.

I want to focus on three aspects of this learning: (1) forming a rich, internal model of the world, (2) self-collection of data, and (3) learning from this continual stream.

Forming a Rich Internal Dynamics Model

From: LeCun, Schmidhuber, Balestriero, Hafner

The big world hypothesis theorizes that because it is highly unlikely that we can model all aspects of the world3, intelligence must build an approximate4 internal model. This model must convert sensory observations—touch, sight, smell, and taste— into physical actions.

Predicting every detail of future observations, as generative world models attempt to do, is computationally expensive and unnecessary for planning. Much of that detail is irrelevant to action, and many aspects of the physical world are difficult or impossible to simulate accurately. That is to say, raw perception is high-dimensional5, noisy, and redundant.

A useful internal model should therefore only preserve structure needed to predict consequences of actions, rather than reproducing the world in full detail. To accomplish that, we map an observation $o_t$ to a latent state $z_t$. Making $z_t$ much lower-dimensional than $o_t$ forces the encoder to be a compressed representation.

$$z_t = f(o_t), \qquad f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^d, \quad d \ll n$$

Our brains do this all the time. Imagine yourself in a coffee shop you frequent. How many chairs are there? Though you've seen all of them, you might not know. Like chairs, leaves on a tree, or the color of your friend's t-shirt, much of perception is abstracted away. Relevant information is contained, and the rest is ignored. Additionally, when goals and intents change, so does the latent information carried (even given the same input). This means that latent states are a goal-conditioned form of perception. We can modify the equation above as follows:

$$z_{t}=f\left(o_{t}\left|\right.g_{}\right)$$

From the set of a given system's actions, we can choose an $a_t$ and use $z_t$ to predict the next state $z_{t+1}$, an idea from classical control theory. This is our forward dynamics model6:

$$\hat{z}_{t+1}=dyna\left(z_{t,}a_{t}\right)$$

$$\hat{z}_{t+1}\sim p_{\theta}\left(z_{t+1}\mid h_{t+1}\right)$$

Planning is simply the process of rolling out trajectories to imagine different futures. A college student can anticipate the sequence of actions that lead to graduation; an athlete can compare the consequences of eating fast food with cooking a healthy meal. In this way, dynamics models serve not only as representations of the world but also as a tool for planning within it.

To learn with this model, we define the loss as the $L2$ distance in latent space between our predicted and observed next state:

$$L=\Vert\hat{z}_{t+1}-z_{t+1}\Vert^2=\Vert^{}dyna\left(f\left(o_{t}\left|\right.g_{}\right)_{,}a_{t}\right)-f\left(z_{t+1}\left|g_{}\right.\right)\Vert^2$$

But learning a useful dynamics model introduces a fundamental problem: representation collapse. If both the perceptual encoder and forward model are trained jointly to minimize prediction error, they can discover a trivial solution. The encoder maps every observation to a constant $c$, and the forward model simply predicts $c$. Prediction error becomes 0, but the learned representation contains no information about the world. There are two broad families of solutions to this problem: contrastive and non-contrastive learning.

Contrastive methods use positive and negative samples to make representations distinguishable.

With InfoNCE loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp\left(\operatorname{sim}\left(z, z^{+}\right) / \tau\right)}{\exp\left(\operatorname{sim}\left(z, z^{+}\right) / \tau\right) + \sum_{i} \exp\left(\operatorname{sim}\left(z, z_{i}^{-}\right) / \tau\right)}$$

However, it becomes increasingly difficult to define useful negatives as the complexity of representation grows, the curse of dimensionality.

Alternatively, non-contrastive methods prevent collapse through a stop-gradient and an optional exponential moving average EMA (BYOL with EMA or SimSiam no EMA):

$$\mathcal{L}=\left\|g\big(f_{\theta}(o_{t}),a_{t}\big)-sg\big(f_{\bar{\theta}}(o_{t+1})\big)\right\|^2$$

$$\bar{\theta}\leftarrow\tau\bar{\theta}+\left(1-\tau\right)\theta,\quad 0\le\tau\le1$$

or alternatively through regularization:

$$L_{\text{total}}=L_{\text{pred}}+\lambda_{}L_{reg}$$

One example found in Barlow Twins and VICReg approaches is dimensionality regularization:

$$\mathcal{L}_{reg_{BT}}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}C_{ij}^2$$

Also constraining the feature space by using an inverse dynamics model:

$$\hat{a}_{t}=g\big(\phi(s_{t}),\,\phi(s_{t+1})\big),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{reg_{inverse}}=\left\|\hat{a}_{t}-a_{t}\right\|_2^2$$

A recent work LeJEPA uses SIGReg as the regularizer:

$$L_{reg_{SIG}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{T}\omega_{k}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\cos\left(t_{k}u_{m}^{\top}z_{i}\right)-e^{-t_{k}^2/2}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sin\left(t_{k}u_{m}^{\top}z_{i}\right)\right)^2$$

Beyond collapse, dynamics models face several other challenges. Latent spaces can distort temporal structure and movement, motivating both temporal straightening and temporal regularization. Long rollouts also accumulate error and make planning expensive, motivating hierarchical models that predict over multiple timescales. Finally, recurrent states can maintain a historical latent state that carries forward information not recoverable from the current observation alone.

Overall, this section is not only an argument for dynamics models but also one against approaches built on human priors, especially language.7

Language is a fixed codebook that humans developed for communication, not necessarily for thought. To produce text, an LLM projects its final hidden state onto a vocabulary, samples one token, and feeds only that token back in. This creates a severe information bottleneck. With a vocabulary size of $V\approx128k$, each token carries at most $\log_{2} V \approx 17$ bits and usually far fewer. A hidden state, by contrast, can contain vastly more information. At $d = 4096$ in bf16 for example, it holds 16d = 65,536 raw bits; even if only a small fraction is meaningful (curse of high dimensions), it still contains hundreds to thousands of useful bits. A dynamics model that carries its state directly forward can therefore preserve information that would require hundreds of tokens—and forward passes—to express. Recent work is already moving in this direction: COCONUT8 reuses the last hidden states instead of sampling tokens, ECHO predicts environment states, and LCM operates in hierarchical latents. Q-labs makes a similar case (in section 2).

But bandwidth is only the shallow problem. Inherently, a model trained to predict text can only learn a world representation shaped by what text contains. Text is a secondhand record of reality: the world is filtered through human perception and then compressed again into words before the model ever sees it. That loss is worst for physical skill, especially knowledge that is difficult to verbalize, like the exact force it takes to crack an egg without crushing it. From a cognitive science perspective, Barbara Tversky in Mind in Motion argues that our internal models of the world are shaped fundamentally by action and spatial experience rather than language. In that view, language is not the substrate of thought but a way of expressing only part of it. It even has a word for what it cannot capture: ineffable.

The strongest counterexample is GPT-6 Astra, which surprised researchers by controlling robot arms with no task-specific training. If Astra learned that capability from language alone, it would be a serious challenge to the argument above: perhaps sufficiently powerful language models can recover far more of the physical world than we expect. But Astra is widely suspected of having seen robot data in training, though OpenAI has not confirmed this. If so, the result points the other way: even the strongest language models need direct physical data.

Either way, both paths remain dependent on data generated and selected by humans, whether text, video, or demonstrations. Moving past that is the subject of this piece’s second argument below.

None of this means abandoning language. It remains an extremely useful interface for communicating instructions. The claim is narrower: language may be a poor substrate for a model's internal state, where continuous latent representations like those used in dynamics models can preserve far more information. Industry momentum may still keep language-centered architectures dominant. But the bitter lesson holds: hand-designed human priors tend to lose to representations learned end to end, and language may be no exception.

Self-Collection of Data Through Exploration*

From: Pathak, Aggarwal, Efros, Schmidhuber

Human knowledge can be thought of as a fractal. Its edges mark the frontier of what is known; each discovery pushes that boundary outward.

We ultimately want systems capable of pushing that frontier outward on their own. But to discover beyond what is already known, a system must first learn to direct its own learning. Today, that choice is still largely made by humans. In pre-training, models learn from a massive corpus that humans have generated, collected, filtered, labeled, and organized.

The first issue is that this data is finite. A recent paper estimates that high-quality internet text could be exhausted within the next couple years (in fact, companies are already extracting text from hardcover books). And the same basic constraint applies even to internet video: there is a finite amount of useful human-generated experience already available.

The main response has been to collect new data through expert demonstrations and egocentric capture, as reflected by a ~$100B current market valuation. But this pipeline remains expensive and labor-intensive. In a recent blog post, Skild AI wrote that for every dollar spent collecting data, it spent roughly three dollars on quality control. It is not scalable to require every additional unit of learning to have a proportional amount of human labor.

Most importantly, the model does not choose what it should learn next. In pre-training, its experience comes from a fixed distribution assembled by humans.9 Deployment-based systems—coding tools, autonomous vehicles, or robots—improve on this by collecting data from their own trajectories. But those trajectories are still generated by a policy optimized for the task at hand, not for learning: the model experiences improved trajectories based on its current task signal. As more samples accumulate from these distributions, their informational value eventually diminishes. What is missing is the ability to actively seek experiences that target the model’s own uncertainty, failures, or gaps in knowledge.

This suggests a hierarchy in how dependent learning is on external experience: (1) finite pre-training data, (2) continuous data generated through static deployment, and (3) agents that actively seek the experiences most useful for improving themselves.

The first two will automate important tasks and create enormous economic value for our labor-constrained economy. Still, the third is the more scalable strategy that will win in the long term.10

Reinforcement learning gets closer to that goal. Instead of learning from a static dataset, an agent interacts with an environment and generates its own experience. Yet, most RL today still relies on task-based reward and environment construction. A robot may be told to pick up a cup, open a drawer, or navigate toward a goal. Its experiences are valuable insofar as they help maximize reward for that particular objective. Learning within the task can certainly generalize, but the exploration itself remains confined by the task. Once the objective changes, a new reward and environment have to be specified. Though the agent determines how to gather data, humans are determining what data is worth gathering.

To remove humans from the loop, it is not enough to allow an agent to act with a task-based reward. The agent must learn to decide what is worth experiencing based on its own current understanding of the world.

Biological intelligence offers a clearer picture of what this might look like. Infant learning is not confined to externally specified tasks, yet it is clearly not random or unguided. Certain experiences intensely capture attention, while others become boring almost immediately.11 At every moment, something internal is evaluating what is worth engaging with.

Again, it is important to emphasize what is worth learning depends on what the learner currently understands. A baby may spend minutes to hours understanding how a ball rolls, while a physicist would learn almost nothing from the same interaction. Conversely, theoretical physics offers little to an infant but can occupy a scientist for decades. Even when seeking help, we naturally choose the person, experiment, or source most likely to resolve the particular gap in our understanding. Useful experience is therefore not absolute; it is crucially conditioned on the learner’s current model of the world.

These signals drive exploration without a predefined endpoint. A fixed dataset is exhausted once it has been consumed, and a task reward becomes irrelevant once the task is solved. Intrinsic learning does not stop at either boundary. As long as some part of the world remains surprising, unpredictable, or poorly understood, the learner can continue generating experiences targeted at that gap.

This is what makes the process "deep" in some sense. A learner does not merely passively observe more examples; it repeatedly proves the same underlying structure from different angles until its consequences become familiar. Alyosha Efros gives an example: close your eyes and imagine what a set of car keys tastes like. Absurd as it sounds, you may have some intuition because, as babies, we did not merely look at objects— we grabbed, rotated, struck, dropped, and even mouthed them.

No one assigned those movements as tasks and there is no reward for specifically discovering how keys taste. Still, the learner probes because there was still something left to understand. Once that interaction became predictable, focus could shift elsewhere.

It is clear that whatever stage of learning— from a baby to a scientist— intelligence needs signals to determine where to direct its computation. What are those internal signals that push the boundary of the fractal outward? Below are a few.

Novelty

A 2018 paper from Burda et al. proposes estimating novelty using Random Network Distillation (RND). The idea is surprisingly simple: train a network $f_{\theta}(x)$ to match the output of a randomly initialized, frozen target network $f_{\phi}(s)$. If a new input lies in or around the previous training input distribution, the error between the network and target is low. Otherwise, it stays high— that error is your novelty signal.

In some sense, RND is a cheap surrogate for density estimation in high-dimensional spaces: the prediction error is a proxy for state rarity, tracking the surprisal $-\log p_{\text{seen}}(x)$. It is important to note that over time this signal fades, so there needs to be some normalization to incentivize revisiting states or the error will drop to 0.

Learning Progress

We can define learning progress by looking at it first through a formal lens through epiplexity. While entropy represents the amount of underlying structure given infinite compute, epiplexity analyzes the structure of data dependent on compute amount.

Consider a random variable $X$ on $\{0,1\}^n$. Let

$$\mathrm{P}^\star = \underset{\mathrm{P} \in \mathcal{P}_{T}}{\arg\min} \left\{ |\mathrm{P}| + \mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{1}{P(X)}\right] \right\}$$

be the program that minimizes the time-bounded MDL, with ties broken by the smallest program and expectations taken over $X$. $|\mathrm{P}|$ is the length of the program $\mathrm{P}$ in bits, and logarithms are base $2$. The $T$-bounded epiplexity $\mathrm{S}_T$ and entropy $\mathrm{H}_T$ of $X$ are

$$\mathrm{S}_T(X) := |\mathrm{P}^\star|, \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{H}_T(X) := \mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{1}{P^\star(X)}\right].$$

It is possible to use it as a regularizer too: https://the-puzzler.github.io/blog/epijepa/

Compression of progress 12 can be thought of as a zeroth-order approximation of epiplexity at a current point in time given data samples formulated by Schmidhuber:

$$LP(x)=\left|\ell_{\text{slow}}(x)-\ell_{\text{fast}}(x)\right|$$

Or a recent work that creates a first-order approximation:

$$r_{i}=\langle g_{i},\delta\theta\rangle$$

Learning progress's advantage over naive prediction error is that it differentiates between an amazing advantage over simple prediction error because if error stays high but it was high before.

A second method from Graves et al. (2017): take one gradient step on $x$ and see how much the loss dropped.

Update: $\theta' = \theta - \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}(x)$. Taylor-expand: $\mathcal{L}_{\theta'}(x) \approx \mathcal{L}_{\theta}(x) - \eta \left\lVert \nabla \mathcal{L}(x) \right\rVert^2$. So $\text{LP}(x) = \mathcal{L}_{\theta}(x) - \mathcal{L}_{\theta'}(x) \approx \eta \left\lVert \nabla \mathcal{L}(x) \right\rVert^2$.

The gradient norm is therefore a cheap LP proxy. Weakness: measuring on the same sample rewards memorization. Graves's "self prediction gain" fixes this by evaluating on a different sample from the same task.

Empowerment

Power is the ability to stay in a reachable state with a large variance/distribution of high EV states. At the same time, it is about making unreachable states reachable. I.e., to have maximum control over the environment

$$E(s)=\max p(a_{1:K})I(A_{1:K};S_{K}\mid s)$$

That $\max_p$ has no closed form. The Mohamed & Rezende trick is a variational lower bound using a learned inverse model $q(a_{1:K} \mid s_K, s)$ ("given where you ended up, guess what you did"):

$$I(A;S_K \mid s) \;\ge\; H(A) + \mathbb{E}\big[\log q(A \mid S_K, s)\big]$$

$I(A;S')=\text{inverse direction }H(A)-H(A\mid S')=\text{forward direction }H(S')-H(S'\mid A)$

Learning Through an Infinite Stream of Data

From: Sutton, Barto

Papers: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.19897

RL through:

  • latent distance

  • distance to goal

  • dense rewards

Learning new information should not overwrite existing knowledge. This is the classic stability—plasticity issue: a model that changes too easily catastrophically forgets old skills, while one that is too constrained loses the ability to adapt.

Modern reinforcement learning handles this mostly through indirect mechanisms. PPO and GRPO constrain policy updates using clipped objectives, limiting how far the new policy can move from the behavior that generated the data. Replay buffers periodically retrain on old experiences, while newer approaches rely on imagined trajectories generated by a learned world model. These methods improve stability but do not determine which parts of the model should remain stable and which should stay plastic.

A more thorough solution is to manage plasticity at the level of individual parameters or neurons. Parameters that contribute strongly to existing behavior can be assigned smaller effective learning rates, while parameters carrying little useful information can change more freely or even be reset.

IDBD, for example, learns a separate step size for each parameter based on whether successive updates remain useful.

Synaptic intelligence estimates parameter importance from how much each weight contributed to reducing loss over its learning trajectory, then penalizes changes to important weights.

Sutskever, on the Dwarkesh Patel podcast, noted that emotions play a vital role in regulating our reaction, perception, and learning. In some sense, a value function serves as a scalar proxy for emotions: high ones signal positive feelings like happiness whereas lower ones mean bad states. A useful plasticity signal may combine prediction error with expected value, so updates are strongest for experiences that are both unexpected and important, rather than merely surprising.

Other methods periodically recycle inactive or low-utility neurons.

Continual learning should not rely on a single global learning rate or a uniformly constrained network. The agent should continually estimate which parts of itself should change in response to new experiences.

Closing Thoughts

The final stack of embodied intelligence will likely include a combination of the three parts above: a regularized hierarchical dynamics model, intrinsic objectives that drive learning, and an architecture that preserves foundational information while being plastic.

Of these, self-collection of data through exploration and self-play seems both the most important and the least emphasized. I hope it becomes a larger focus of the field.

Though these ideas are framed in the context of physical intelligence, they should extend to autonomous systems more broadly, including those operating on the internet.13

A quote from one of my favorite shows encapsulates the main idea of this essay well:

“Anyone can memorize facts and figures, but to really LEARN something, you’ve got to go out and do it yourself… follow your curiosity!” - the man with the yellow hat
