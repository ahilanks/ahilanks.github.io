Experiential Learning in Embodied Intelligence

Inspired from biological intelligence

Sep 22, 26 · 12 min

“The most wonderful thing about [children] is their infinite capacity for wonder”— Alison Gopnik

The Bitter Lesson

Last November on the Dwarkesh Patel podcast, Richard Sutton explained the Bitter Lesson, arguing that methods for intelligence leveraging human data are consistently overtaken by those built on a system’s own experience.

Even at scale, approaches that rely primarily on existing data face a fundamental ceiling. Human data is finite (we will eventually run out), and producing more of it is slow and expensive. More importantly, each additional data sample is not generated to maximize model learning, relying on human aggregation and filtering. In contrast, experiential learning methods generate own data through exploration and search. As a result, its progress is bounded only by compute, which has historically proven to scale far better and thus a much better strategy for intelligence.

In chess, AlphaGo, trained on the games of grandmasters, was beaten by AlphaZero, an experience based method which learned by playing, losing, and winning. In Atari, methods built upon the rules of the game were beaten by experience based methods.

In natural language, LLMs initially improved with the scale of data and model parameters. But now that most internet data (and even text from hardcover books) has been consumed, research has returned to reinforcement and test-time learning. And this pattern, where experience vastly outperforms data, has repeated itself across image processing, translation, and scientific understanding.

Still, I don't think this lesson is understood, especially in embodied intelligence. In academic labs and industry, much of the hype surrounds methods built on human-derived priors like language or collecting human data through teleoperation, videos, and egocentric capture. Many of these methods will solve important tasks for automation and manufacturing; but if the goal is general physical intelligence1— not just competence on certain, limited tasks— then these approaches are the exact trap Sutton warns about.

Animals Play

Young animals offer a useful example of experiential learning. They learn from exploration: playing, fighting, moving, falling, and trying new things.

A natural counterpoint against this is that many animals, like horses or giraffes, can walk almost immediately after birth. Indeed, this suggests the opposite of self-exploration: that genes encode a kind of “pre-training.” However, these built-in behaviors are primarily typical of animals with lower encephalization quotient (EQ), or brain to body size. Low EQ animals are ready to act early, but their behavior is less flexible. On the other hand, animals2 with higher EQ like chimpanzees and dogs are the opposite. They are born far more helpless and dependent upon their guardians for longer, but gain the ability to adapt, generalize, and learn from experience.

For more evidence, please look at this website. This is only a correlation, not evidence of a causal relationship, but I think it points to an interesting trend in nature: greater behavioral flexibility seems to come with greater dependence on learning from experience.

And importantly, this tradeoff isn’t neutral. Humans, at the extreme end of high EQ, dominate in general intelligence. Human infants are born with very little hardcoded behavior and are vulnerable for years, but with a brain designed to learn rapidly and flexibly.

tl;dr: nature also supports the bitter lesson.

Babies Self-Collect to Learn

The most general learners among young animals are human babies. They are born with little3 control over their bodies or environment, yet they learn to build motor skills, recognize patterns, and predict outcomes.

I want to focus on three aspects of this learning: (1) forming a rich, internal model of the world, (2) self-collection of data, and (3) learning from this continual stream.

Forming an Rich Internal Dynamics Model

From: LeCunn, Schmidhuber, Balastario

The big world hypothesis theorizes that because it is highly unlikely that we can model all aspects of the world4, intelligence must build an approximate5 internal model.

This internal model must convert sensory observations— touch, sight, smell, and taste— into physical actions. Because raw perception is high-dimensional6, containing noise and redundancy, mapping directly from perception to physical actions directly is compute-heavy and doesn't allow for planning.

The solution is to create a function that maps an observation $o_t$ to an intermediate latent state representation $z_t$. Defining a lower dimensional $z_t$ relative to perception forces it to be a compressed function of observations.

$$z_{t}=f(o_{t})$$

Our brains do this all the time. Imagine yourself a coffee shop you frequent. How many chairs are there? Though you've see all of them, you might not know. Like chairs, much of perception is abstracted away. Relevant information is contained, while everything else is ignored. And when intents and goals change, so does latent information, even given the same observation. That is to say, latent states are a goal-conditioned form of perception. We can modify the equation above as follows:

$$z_{t}=f\left(o_{t}\left|\right.g_{}\right)$$

From the set of a given system's actions, we can choose an $a_t$ and use $z_t$ to predict the next state $z_{t+1}$. This is our forward dynamics model7:

$$\hat{z}_{t+1}=dyna\left(z_{t,}a_{t}\right)$$

Planning is simply the process of rolling out trajectories to imagine different futures. A college student can anticipate the sequence of actions that lead to graduation; an athlete can compare the consequences of eating fast food with cooking a healthy meal. In this way, dynamics models serves not only as representations of the world but also as tools for planning within it.

To learn with this model, we define the loss below, comparing the difference in latent space between our prediction and actual next state:

$$L=\Vert\hat{z}_{t+1}-z_{t+1}\Vert^2$$

$$=\Vert^{}dyna\left(f\left(o_{t}\left|\right.g_{}\right)_{,}a_{t}\right)-f\left(z_{t+1}\left|g_{}\right.\right)\Vert^2$$

But learning a useful dynamics model introduces a fundamental problem: representation collapse. If both the perceptual encoder and dynamics model and trained jointly to minimize prediction error, they can discover a trivial solution. The encoder maps every observation to the same constant $c$, while the dynamics model simply predicts $c$ at every step. Prediction error becomes 0, but the learned representation contains no information about the world.

There are two broad families of solutions to this problem: contrastive and non-contrastive learning.

Contrastive methods uses positive and negative samples to make representations distinguishable, with InfoNCE loss:

$$L=-\log \frac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z,z^{+})/\tau)+\sum_i \exp(\operatorname{sim}(z,z_i^{-})/\tau)}{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z,z^{+})/\tau)}$$

Examples include Barlow Twins and Siamese-style approaches. However, it becomes increasingly difficult to define useful negative as the dimensionality and complexity of the data grows, the curse of dimensionality.

That is why contrastive methods are preferred, instead preventing collapse through architectural constraints like exponential moving average (EMA) and stop-grad

$$\xi \leftarrow \tau \xi + (1 - \tau)\theta$$

or regularization

$$L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{pred}} + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \cdot R(Z)$$

A recent work LeJEPA uses SIGReg as the regularizer:

$$L_{\mathrm{SIGReg}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{T}\omega_{k}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\cos\left(t_{k}u_{m}^{\top}z_{i}\right)-e^{-t_{k}^{2}/2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sin\left(t_{k}u_{m}^{\top}z_{i}\right)\right)^{2}$$

In addition to this model, we also need some recurrence to satisfy an MDP or POMDP setup: LSTM & RSSM SSM

I'm not only arguing for dynamics models but also against other methods for representation.

The primary alternate approach is using language as an intermediate representation, either through a Vision Language Model (VLM) or coding models with robotic controls as another tool. This is inefficient and unnecessary. Language is a low-dimensional discretized form of representation, created for communication not reasoning, especially in the physical world. Professor Jitendra Malik articulates that "to do low level control with LLMs sound perversely inefficient and unnecessary".

In Mind in Motion, Barbara Tversky explains that it is action not language that forms our internal model of the world. Babies think in complex ways long before they learn language. And after we learn language, thoughts are still much richer than language. It explains why even those who are brilliant struggle to convert thoughts into words. And it explains why you can think of a thought far faster than you can write it. Mathematically, a latent state can represent much more information than a discrete token, or any combination thereof8.

Though useful for other purposes like entertainment, 3D reconstruction or segmentation is also an unnecessary, lossy intermediate representation for physical intelligence, as argued by Jon Barron.

Because of the current momentum in industry around LLMS, language may become the dominant form of representation. However, the argument remains that language is a lossy, inefficient form of representation.

Self-Collection of Data Through Exploration

From: Hafner, Pathak, Aggarwal, Efros, Schmidhuber

Human knowledge can be thought of as a fractal. The edges mark the frontier of what is known; each new discovery pushes that edge outward.

We ultimately want systems capable of pushing that frontier outward on their own. To discover beyond the frontier, it must first learn to structure its own learning. But today, that choice is still largely made by humans.

In pre-training, models learn from massive corpora that humans have generated, collected filtered, labeled, and organized. Even when the learning objective itself is self-supervised, the model does not decide what information it needs next. Its experience by a fixed distribution that humans have already assembled.9

More importantly the data pipeline remains expensive. If intelligence is to scale primarily through experience, it is undesirable for every additional unit of learning to require proportional amounts of human collection, filtering, and quality control. Skild, for example, recently wrote that for every dollar it sepnt collecting data, it spent roughly three dollars on quality control. The deeper bottleneck is not only the amount of data available, but the amount of human labor required to decide what data is worth collecting. Human-collected data is therefore expensive, finite, and suboptimal for learning: it is gathered according to what humans produce and choose to record, not what would maximize the model’s learning.

Reinforcement learning gets closer to autonomous data collection. Instead of learning only from a static dataset, an agent interacts with an environments and generates its own experience. Yet most RL today still relies on another form of human supervision, through the task-based reward and environment construction. A robot may be told to pick up a cup, open a drawer, or navigate toward a goal. Its experiences are valuable insofar as they help maximize reward for that particular objective. Learning within the task can certainly generalize, but the exploration itself remains confined by the task. Once the objective changes, a new reward has to be specified.

Moreover, someone still has to define the task, design the reward, construct the environment, and decide what kinds of experiences should matter. The agent may determine how to gather data, but humans are still largely determining what data is worth gathering.

If we want to remove humans from the loop, then allowing an agent to act is not enough. The agent must also learn to decide what is worth experiencing.

Biological intelligence appears to solve this problem. Humans do not explore the world through explicit tasks. Even month old babies are intensely captivated by some experiences while quickly bored by others (Gopnik 27). As they gain the ability to move and manipulate their surroundings, they increasingly shape their environment around those interests.

Alyosha Efros gives a useful example. If you close your eyes and imagine what a set of car keys taste like, you may have an immediate intuition. As babies, we explored objectives through every available modality. We looked at them, grabbed, them, rotated them, hit them against surfaces, and even put them in our mouths. This can be thought of thought of as a form of "deep data." Rather than observing an enormous number of independent examples, children repeatedly interrogate parts of the world until they feel like they have understood them.

At whatever stage of learning— from a baby to a scientist— intelligence needs signals to determine where to spend time and energy. What are those internal signals that push the boundary of the fractal outward? Below are a few.

Novelty

A 2018 paper from Burda et. al proposes estimating novelty using Random Network Distillation (RND). The idea is surprisingly simple: train a network $f_{\theta}(x)$ to match the output of a randomly initialized, frozen target network $f_{\phi}(s)$. If a new input lies in or around the previous training input distribution, the error between the and target is low. Otherwise, it stays high— that error is your novelty signal.

In some sense, RND is a cheap surrogate for density estimation in high dimension spaces: the prediction error is a proxy for state rarity, tracking the surprisal $-\log p_{\text{seen}}(x)$.

Closing Thoughts

The final stack of embodied intelligence will most likely include a combination of the above. A regularized hierarchical dynamics model, pushed to act with intrinsic desires that maximize learning, and a model architecture that preserves foundational information while being plastic.

Though these ideas have been discussed in the context of physical intelligence, they should extend to autonomous systems in general, including ones operating on the internet.

A quote from one of my favorite shows encapsulates the main idea of this essay well:

“Anyone can memorize facts and figures, but to really LEARN something, you’ve got to go out and do it yourself… follow your curiosity!” - the man with the yellow hat
