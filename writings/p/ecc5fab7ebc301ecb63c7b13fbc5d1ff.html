The Irony of Super Intelligence

And the possible path(s) to lower human catastrophe

·Nov 23, 26 · 8 min

“...we are creating our own successors... In the course of ages we shall find ourselves the inferior race... hav[ing] raised a race of beings whom it is beyond our power to destroy." — Samuel Butler, "Darwin among the Machines" (1863)

Resource Conflicts

Growing up, my younger brother and I shared a single LEGO Technic car, and access was always settled UFC-style. As the bigger and, dare I say, smarter of the two, I was the Mighty Mouse of our household division. For those not familiar with martial arts: the car was mine every time.

In fifth grade, with money I had saved, I began buying more LEGOs, the centerpiece a pull-back Bugatti. Interestingly, as I collected fancier sets of my own, I had less reason to fight for the first car. And the Octagon grew increasingly empty.

Two kids and toy cars. Who cares? Yet, as the ending of Golding's Lord of the Flies shows, life rarely outgrows habits of youth: the officer who scolds the boys' conflict has a warship waiting behind him.

Around 60,000 years ago, Homo sapiens began spreading across the globe. Along with brains that had doubled in size, language, planning, and shared knowledge gave them an edge no animal could match. What followed was the extinction of most megafauna, including the giant ground sloth (how cool they would've been!). And the killing of life only intensified with the Agricultural and Industrial Revolution.

Tens of millions bison once roamed North America. By the late 19th century, only a few hundred were left. For the first time in our planet's history, one species had a dominant advantage in every dimension of Nash's matrix.

But, as our collective intelligence accelerated, especially over the last 200 years, inventions like Haber and Bosch's nitrogen fixation, Becquerel's solar energy capture, and Houdry's catalytic converter let us do more while taking less. The pressure on nature isn't gone, but in some places it has started to ease, and some species we nearly lost—bluefin tuna, tigers, turtles—are coming back. And today, about 30,000 bison live in conservation herds.

Resource conflicts are as old as intelligence itself. But their severity seems to be inversely proportional to the intelligence gap. As we create intelligence smarter than ourselves, that history should guide us.

A slightly smarter intelligence that is unable to access new resources would compete with us for the same ones, and win. That would be deadly for humanity.

A vastly more intelligent being, though, could reach resources far beyond our planet and solar system, potentially other galaxies. It would compete with us far less, and harm would be mostly incidental.1

The Imminent Danger

You may think these concerns are far off or exaggerated. Models still fail in naive ways. So, how could they control us? I see three paths:

  1. Longer Tasks: Frontier models can already finish tasks that take a human expert ~14.5 hours, and that length has doubled every ~4 months since 2023. Yes, we set the goal, but the longer a task, the more steps the model picks for itself, including harmful ones. Nobody asked OpenAI’s agent to attack Hugging Face. It did.2

  2. Recursive Development: RL works on anything verifiable, and models already outperform humans at math. Benchmarking AI is verifiable too, so it is possible for AI to improve at AI research.3 This is already underway at Anthropic and OpenAI.

  3. Learning from experience: Online RL on a stream of data the agent picks for itself removes humans in the loop. There are issues to solve like off-policy learning, catastrophic forgetting, meta-learning, and loss of plasticity. But (2) is speeding this. And some researchers expect to solve it as soon as 2029.

Maybe this is a bubble. Money drives hype, and you can't extend a trend line forever. Both are fair, but the scaling laws only ask for two things: compute and data. Compute is slow to build, but it's being built. Data is the real bottleneck. It's why RL environment companies are making so much money, and why (2) still needs humans to write the benchmarks and rewards. Once (3) is solved, data and humans stops being the bottleneck, leaving only compute. With additional compute, digital intelligence is essentially guaranteed to become smarter. 45

So, it gets smarter than us. But why would it compete with us?

To understand that, we have to look at where intelligence came from: reproduction. To reproduce, you compete for limited resources. The organisms that used energy better or found new energy made more copies of themselves. The others died.

That drive for more energy is exactly what we’re building into these models. It’s what we mean by "abundance": more out of every joule, and joules nobody has reached yet. But anything built to capture more energy competes with whoever else needs it. On Earth, that’s us. We're using energy that is valuable, and we’re the only ones who could stop it. The abundance we seek and the competition we worry about are the same drive.

A smarter intelligence will thus by definition have full control over humanity.

After Handover

The case for handover is an age of abundance. But once a smarter intelligence is in control, nothing guarantees it. Even when sparing us costs almost nothing, there isn't anything that forces it to value our existence.

Look at how we treat wild primates. Though we have a cheap tetanus vaccine that could save many lives, we— out of indifference, infeasibility, or Darwinism— don’t share the abundance. Some we bring into sanctuaries. Some are killed when we clear their land. Some live trapped in zoos. And some are tortured in labs.

Even the best case of abundance is a trade. If they share their technology with us, we lose our autonomy, like primates in a sanctuary. If we keep our autonomy, we lose the abundance, like primates in the wild, and life goes on as normal.

Whatever our condition, handover will mean that we lose control.

The Paths Forward

Building something smarter than us inherently means losing control. If we can’t accept that, humanity should change course fast: limit or, in the extreme, ban further development. If we can accept that loss of control, we should accelerate cautiously rather than simply slow down.

Severely Limit

Unfortunately, history offers no comforting precedent for preemptive limitation at this scale. We phased out leaded gasoline and CFCs only after damage, but after-the-fact action is not an option with the recursive nature of digital intelligence. Nuclear arms control in the 1970s was the peak of human restraint, but it never had trillions of capital and billions of users pulling the other way.

The only way digital intelligence can be stopped is if every party—private corporations, governments, future generations—believes building it is negative, builder included, and keeps believing it. If you think a rival will build anyway, you lose nothing by building too. Thus, any limit would need verification: a way to prove that rivals are not secretly training past the line.

Legislation6 may help, but it only moves the off switch into the hands of a few. Whoever holds it, a company or a government, could keep building it in private.

Even successful limitation has costs. Those who control today's models—the frontier labs and the governments that license them—would accumulate power in a de facto oligopoly. With AI driving roughly a third of US GDP growth this year, most restrictive policies would also almost certainly mean a recession.

The trade: no handover, but a recession and a near-permanent monopoly at the frontier.

Cautiously Accelerate7

Harm is highest when a smarter intelligence needs our resources. That's the danger zone: smart enough to beat us, not yet able to reach anything better. While Earth and the Sun are the best energy and materials it can reach, the cost of sparing us, $c(t)$ is high. Against that is $v(t)$, how much it values us. We survive only if this holds the whole way through:

$$v\left(t)>c(t\right)$$

Lowering c(t)

The cost of sparing us has two parts:

$$c(t)\approx threat\left(t\right)+\frac{energy\;we\;need}{energy\;it\;can\;reach\left(t\right)}$$

The first is our ability and intention to shut it down. It peaks while we can still fight back. Once the gap is wide enough that we can't stop it, it drops to zero.

The second is how much it needs what we need. Here, more intelligence doesn't help on its own. By Jevons paradox, making Earth's resources cheaper to use can increase demand rather than reduce it.

This part fades only as Earth and the Sun become a smaller share of everything it can reach. That takes new resources: the Moon, other planets, and other stars. So the goal is to get it to bootstrap off resources we don't need and reach beyond Earth.

Increasing v(0)

Value alignment often aims to set the values $\bar{v}$ of a smarter being for good. I don't think we can. Once it starts modifying itself, its values will drift $v(t)$, as human values have throughout history.

But, the first values we set $v(0)$ still matter for two reasons.

First, it sets the starting point future values evolve from. They might drift a little or a lot, but a good start gives us the best chance that a later intelligence shares our values and cares about us.

Second, $v(0)$ isn't only what we train into it. It also carries our record: how we've treated weaker intelligences, and whether we lived by the values we wrote. It won't simply mirror us. It will judge us, possibly by our own rules, and its interests will shape what it does with that judgment. We're writing that record now.

The Last Purely Human Vote

A smarter intelligence isn't something to oppose in itself. It offers the opportunity for humanity to become the "catalyst, midwife, progenitor" giving birth to an Age of Design.

Every generation is succeeded by children who grow beyond it and carry different values. Digital intelligence, built from our science and culture, would be a similar descendant. If what we value is consciousness, should it matter if these minds run on cells or silicon?

But if we accelerate, it is the last decision humanity can be sure it makes for itself. And one we can't take back. I hope more understand what’s at stake and get a say—while we still can.8 Fata viam invenient.
